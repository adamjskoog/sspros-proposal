03 / THE AI EVALUATION

Tested before
it’s trusted.

Most AI projects fail quietly: a confident wrong answer, a promise nobody approved, a cost nobody tracked. So I built the triage step first and measured it against hand-labeled inquiries before proposing it.

  1. 01Untrusted input

    Customer text is wrapped and treated as data, never as instructions.

  2. 02Structured extraction

    Claude returns an 11-field handoff that must match a strict schema.

  3. 03Fixed rules

    Routing rules and approved facts live in one versioned prompt.

  4. 04Human approval

    Nothing reaches a customer without a person signing off.

  5. 05Log & measure

    Corrections, cost, and model and prompt versions are recorded.

What could go wrong

Each risk has a control, and each control is tested or enforced.

A missed emergency
Escalation runs first and outranks every other rule. Emergency recall is a release gate.
An invented price or promise
Drafts use approved facts only, are scanned automatically, and need approval to send.
Prompt injection through the form
Customer text is treated as data. Attack cases are part of every test run.
Customer data exposure
Send only the inquiry text, use a business API account that excludes training on submitted data, and test with redacted examples.
Silent quality drift
The model and prompt are pinned. Rerun the evaluation before any change ships.
Runaway cost or an outage
Per-request limits, a cached prompt, a monthly cap, and the manual process as the fallback.
BUY BEFORE BUILD

First, check what the team’s existing tools already do: form routing, CRM rules, email templates. Build only the part they can’t do well, which is reading a free-text request, and keep it inside the tools the team already uses.

More responsibility as it works.

Days 1–30 · Learn & deliver

Get the foundations right.

Learn how inquiries are handled today, agree the first assignments, and measure a baseline.

WHAT I’LL DELIVER
  • An approved public facts sheet and a ranked correction list.
  • Revised homepage copy and two priority service-page drafts.
  • A map of the inquiry process, with owners and handoffs.
  • A baseline for response time, missing details, and handling effort.
Review at day 30

Are the drafts accurate and useful? Which workflow is worth improving first?

Days 31–60 · Own & test

Own the recurring work.

Take over two routines, then test AI triage on the company’s own data before anyone relies on it.

WHAT I’LL DELIVER
  • Two maintained routines: public-content upkeep and inquiry follow-up.
  • Three adapted templates and an inquiry-form specification with a location field.
  • The evaluation rerun on approved, redacted real inquiries, with results reviewed together.
  • Agreed release targets, data handling rules, and a monthly spend cap.
Review at day 60

Does AI triage meet the targets on real inquiries? If not, what would it take?

Days 61–90 · Pilot & decide

Show the results. Choose what’s next.

Run a limited pilot alongside the existing process and measure it against the baseline.

WHAT I’LL DELIVER
  • A limited AI-assisted triage pilot, with every reply approved by a person.
  • A before-and-after review covering time, corrections, and cost.
  • Short operating instructions, a backup owner, and a manual fallback.
  • A recommendation to expand, adjust, or stop, plus a next-quarter backlog.
Review at day 90

Which responsibilities become permanent? Agree the ongoing role, scope, and compensation.

Four measures worth watching.

Response time

Median time from receipt to the first human response.

Handoff completeness

Share of requests that arrive with the essential details.

Follow-up coverage

Open requests with an owner and a dated next action.

Handling effort

Minutes per request, including review and corrections.
